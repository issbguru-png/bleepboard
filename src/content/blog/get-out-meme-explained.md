Ask where the viral **"GET OUT"** scream comes from and the internet answers in unison: Tuco Salamanca, the volatile kingpin from the early seasons of *Breaking Bad*, played by Raymond Cruz. The sound is named after him, tagged after him, and cut into thousands of edits of his face. One problem with all of that. It isn't him.

## The answer everyone gives

You can see why the attribution stuck. Tuco is one of television's most memorably unstable villains, a man whose mood flips from celebration to violence inside a single breath, and a throat-shredding "GET OUT" sounds exactly like something he would produce. But go looking for the scene and you will not find it. Tuco never screams this line in *Breaking Bad*. There is no episode to clip, and [Know Your Meme's entry on the sound](https://knowyourmeme.com/memes/tuco-get-out-scream) files the whole thing under misattribution.

## Where the scream actually comes from

The real source, as best anyone has traced it, is a livestream of **Paradise State Roleplay**, a *Grand Theft Auto V* roleplay server founded in the summer of 2021. In the original clip, a player drives a Lightning McQueen monster truck up to an in-game car meet and asks whether he can have a spot. The scream is the answer he gets. The clip circulated through the server's own TikTok account, which has since been deleted, and reposts of it were doing numbers by spring 2023.

So who actually says it? Nobody knows. The voice belongs to some unnamed roleplayer whose identity never travelled with the audio, and at this point it probably never will. That is the honest answer to the meme's most-Googled question: the most recognisable scream on the internet is anonymous.

## How a resemblance joke became a fact

The Tuco connection has a precise birthday. On 10 May 2023, an iFunny user called flyingmantis posted the clip with the caption "GET OUT. Bro sounded like TUCO from Breaking Bad." Read that carefully: *sounded like*. It was a comparison, and a good one. What happened next is the interesting part, because every retelling shaved a little off the qualifier until nothing was left.

Reposts carried the clip to TikTok within weeks. Then in March 2024 the creator **@notkchaze** started building videos around the sound, beginning with a drive-thru clip captioned "why is tuco working at mcdonalds" that pulled millions of views, followed by a "Salamanca Sliders" series that did even bigger numbers. Those videos were jokes that treated the attribution as true, and to anyone arriving cold, a joke that assumes a fact looks like confirmation of it. Layer enough of those on top of each other and the resemblance gag hardens into received wisdom. No one person invented the false origin. The crowd did, one dropped "sounded like" at a time, and [KYM's editorial on the question](https://knowyourmeme.com/editorials/guides/where-is-the-get-out-scream-from-and-is-it-really-tuco-salamanca-the-tiktok-meme-sound-explained) had to be written because the correction now travels slower than the myth.

## Why the sound works no matter who says it

None of this hurts the clip, because the clip never needed the backstory. It is about a second long with no run-up at all. It starts at maximum and stays there, the vowel breaking into rasp and distortion, and distortion is the thing your ear reads as *out of control*. A shout can still be a performance. This doesn't sound performed.

TikTok editors deploy it as a punchline ejector seat: build up any scenario, an opinion nobody asked for, a player doing something cursed, an animal misbehaving, then hit the GET OUT and cut. The scream is wildly disproportionate to whatever you attach it to, and the gap between the tiny offence and the thermonuclear response is the joke. An anonymous GTA player supplied a ceiling that few sounds on the internet can match.

## The rest of the ejection family

The scream has competition, and the differences are worth knowing because they aren't interchangeable.

- [You Need to Leave](/sound/you-need-to-leave/) is the same instruction delivered with total composure, which somehow lands worse than shouting.
- [SHUT UP](/sound/shut-up/) targets the sentence rather than the person. Use it when someone should stop talking but is welcome to stay.
- [Hell Naw Dog](/sound/hell-naw/) and [Nuh Uh](/sound/nuh-uh/) are refusals rather than evictions. Nobody's going anywhere; the idea is just dead.
- [Bruh](/sound/bruh/) is what you play when the offence is beneath a reaction this size.
