Nobody has ever slept through one. The alert lands, the phone makes a noise it makes at no other time, and every person in the room reaches for a pocket at once. That reaction is engineered, and most of the engineering happened decades before anyone was carrying a phone.

## What the Amber Alert Sound Actually Is

The tone is an attention signal, and it is a separate object from the message it introduces. Wireless Emergency Alerts, the system that carries Amber Alerts in the United States, went live in April 2012. It does not send you a text. Alerts travel by cell broadcast, which pushes a single message out to every handset attached to a tower rather than addressing phones one at a time, so a full stadium receives the same words in the same instant without the network folding.

What your handset plays on receipt is built from the attention signal used on radio and television. That borrowing is why the sound has its strange quality of not belonging to your phone at all.

## Where the Two-Tone Signal Came From

The broadcast signal is old and rigidly specified. Federal rules require the Emergency Alert System attention signal to be made of two fundamental frequencies, 853 Hz and 960 Hz, transmitted simultaneously and held within half a hertz of those values.

Those two numbers are almost exactly a major second apart. That interval is close enough to grind rather than blend, so the ear takes the result as roughness instead of a chord, and familiarity never sands it down. The signal was specified this way on purpose. It has to work through a noisy kitchen, a car radio and a bad speaker, and it has to be unmistakable on a listener who is not paying attention.

The harsh digital screech that often runs ahead of it on television is a different thing again. That burst is data, not decoration: it carries the machine-readable header telling every receiver down the chain what kind of alert this is, which counties it covers and how long it lasts.

## Why It Beats Your Silent Switch

Emergency alerts sound even when your ringer is off, and that is the part people find most startling. The logic behind it is simple enough. A silent switch is a statement about which people you want to hear from, and an evacuation order is not a person.

Your alarm clock gets a comparable exemption for comparable reasons. The friendlier version of the same argument is the [iPhone alarm sound](/blog/iphone-alarm-sound-explained/), a tone designed to be inescapable while still being one you picked yourself.

## Who Amber Was

AMBER is a backronym. Officially the letters stand for America's Missing: Broadcast Emergency Response, but they were chosen for Amber Hagerman, a nine-year-old abducted in Arlington, Texas on 13 January 1996 and found murdered four days later. In the weeks afterwards, callers to Dallas radio stations suggested that broadcasters could push urgent bulletins about missing children the same way they pushed weather warnings. Local radio managers and police built the first version of the system out of that idea. Her case has never been solved.

## Why You Cannot Put It in an Advert

Federal regulations make it unlawful to transmit the EAS or WEA attention signals, or anything close enough that an ordinary listener might mistake it for the real thing, outside an actual emergency or an authorised test. The rule covers adverts, promos and ordinary programming, and the FCC has fined broadcasters for crossing it. The reasoning matches the reason the tone works at all: a warning sound holds its meaning only while it is never used for anything else.

That restriction is about broadcasting. Playing the clip to your flatmate sits outside it, although the reaction you get will be hard to tell apart.

## Can You Turn Amber Alerts Off?

Most alert types, yes. Amber Alerts, imminent threat alerts and public safety alerts can each be switched off in your phone's notification settings. National alerts issued by the President or the head of FEMA are the exception, and handsets are not permitted to offer a switch for those at all.
