Somewhere in the world right now, a teacher is writing a number on a whiteboard and a room full of eleven-year-olds is losing its mind. The number is 67. If you're an adult trying to work out what on earth is happening, here's the good news: you are not missing anything, because there is nothing to miss.

## What 67 actually means

Nothing at all. That isn't a dodge or a shrug on my part: it is the actual answer, and the joke is built on top of it.

"Six seven" is always said as two separate numbers, never "sixty-seven". It's pure verbal confetti. It isn't code for a rude word, it isn't a reference you're too old to get, and it doesn't stand for anything. Ask a child to define it and watch them genuinely struggle, because the honest definition is closer to a noise than a word. It works the way "wassup" or a fist bump works: a social signal that costs nothing and means only *I am here and you are here*.

The emptiness is the appeal. Every previous generation had slang that adults could eventually decode and then ruin by using it. 67 can't be decoded, so it can't be ruined. When a parent says "six seven" at the dinner table the joke doesn't transfer, it just dies, and the dying is funnier to the kid watching than the joke ever was.

## Where it came from

The audio traces back to the Skrilla track **"Doot Doot (6 7)"**, where the phrase repeats as a hook. Like a lot of rap hooks it escaped the song almost immediately and went looking for footage to attach itself to.

It found basketball. Editors started pairing the "six seven" hook with highlight clips, and the number picked up a second, sort-of-plausible meaning on the way: 6'7" is a very common height for a basketball player, so anybody listed at six-foot-seven became instant meme fuel. Whether the height connection came first or got reverse-engineered by kids trying to justify the joke to adults is genuinely unclear. Both readings coexist quite happily now, so nobody has bothered to settle it.

Then it made the jump every truly viral meme has to make, from screens into rooms. It became a **call and response**. One person says "six", the room says "seven". Nothing more to it than that. Once a meme becomes a chant it stops needing the internet to survive, and this one went through schools worldwide faster than any platform could have carried it.

## The hand gesture and the maths trap

The chant travels with a gesture. Hands held out flat, palms up, alternating up and down like a pair of scales weighing two invisible options. It reads as a shrug, an *eh, could be this, could be that*, which suits a phrase that means nothing at all.

Then there's the maths variant. Because 67 is a perfectly ordinary number it turns up in classrooms constantly by accident, and kids quickly learned to engineer it on purpose. Sum a string of numbers so the total lands on 67 and the class erupts. Teachers report setting an innocent arithmetic question and realising, one second too late, what the answer is going to be. Maths lessons became a minefield. Some teachers have banned the number outright, which is the single funniest possible outcome and a guarantee of another year of it.

## Why adults find it so annoying

Meme-literate adults can usually meet Gen Alpha halfway. This one refuses to be met. There's no punchline to appreciate, no origin story that makes it click, nothing clever hiding underneath. It's a sound a child enjoys making, repeated at volume, for months on end. No amount of explaining improves it. The frustration adults feel is itself part of the content, too: every viral clip of a teacher pleading for it to stop is another reason to keep going.

It got big enough that dictionary publishers took notice, which is traditionally the sign that a piece of slang has peaked and is about to become deeply uncool. That day is coming. Until then: six.
