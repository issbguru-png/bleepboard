"Nothing beats a Jet2 holiday. And right now, you can save £50 per person…" If you can already hear the relentlessly sunny voice and the pop track underneath it, congratulations: you've been on TikTok during one of the great absurdist meme runs of recent years.

## What the audio actually is

The clip comes from a real British TV advert for **Jet2holidays**, the UK package-holiday company. There's an upbeat backing song and a honey-smooth voiceover promising savings on your next getaway. By every measure it is a perfectly ordinary advert, and the ordinariness is what made it such good raw material.

The song underneath is Jess Glynne's "Hold My Hand", which had already been a hit years before it was ever attached to a holiday brochure. That matters more than it sounds. Half the audience knew the chorus without ever having watched the advert, so the audio arrived pre-loaded with a warm, uncomplicated, summer-holiday feeling, and the meme got to spend all of it in one go.

The clip runs about eleven seconds, though most edits never get that far. Almost everything happens in the first line.

## The meme format

TikTok's twist was simple and devastating. Play the cheerful ad audio over footage of holidays going catastrophically wrong. A wasp invasion at the beach bar. A parasailing mishap. Someone's suitcase exploding on the carousel. A hotel corridor flooding. And over all of it, that serene voice: *"Nothing beats a Jet2 holiday."*

The comedy engine is **tonal dissonance**. The bigger the disaster on screen, the harder the chirpy narration hits. It's the same mechanism as playing elevator music during a fistfight, but with a specificity that made it feel fresh: everyone has been on that holiday where things went sideways, and everyone recognises the gap between the brochure and the reality.

The format went so viral that the voiceover artist behind the ad became a minor celebrity, and the phrase escaped the internet entirely. You'll now hear "nothing beats a Jet2 holiday" said out loud, in person, whenever anything goes wrong near a swimming pool.

## Why indifference is funnier than commentary

Most disaster sounds react to the disaster. The [Oh No No No laugh](/sound/oh-no-no-no/) starts wheezing before the impact even lands, and [It Was at This Moment He Knew](/sound/he-knew/) arrives specifically to point at the mistake. Both are commentary. Both are on your side.

The Jet2 audio does not know anything has happened. It carries on selling a family break at £50 off while a gazebo cartwheels down a beach behind it, and that refusal to acknowledge the footage is the whole trick. It's closer to what [elevator music](/sound/elevator-music/) does in an awkward silence: cheerfulness that keeps going long after the room has stopped deserving it. Nothing in the audio is doing comedy. The clash is, and the clash gets funnier the harder the edit works to earn it.

## Why it stuck

Most audio memes burn out in weeks. This one lasted because the supply of holiday disasters is infinite and self-renewing. Every summer produces a fresh harvest of chaos footage and the audio is sitting there waiting for it — seasonal and evergreen at once, and deeply British with it.

It also survives leaving the internet, which very few meme sounds manage. The phrase is a complete sentence with a built-in ironic use, so it works spoken aloud in an airport queue by someone who has never opened the app. A meme that can travel by voice doesn't need a platform to keep it alive.

## Making one of these edits

Start the audio over the calm shot, not the chaos. The hotel exterior, the pool at eight in the morning, the family walking through departures with their whole holiday still in front of them. Let the voiceover get a few words in before anything goes wrong, because the joke needs the advert to be winning first.

Then leave the audio alone. The instinct to cut the music when the disaster lands is exactly the wrong one. Hold the line to its end and let the £50 offer play out over the wreckage.
