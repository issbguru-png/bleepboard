One siren, several different sounds, and the switching between them is not an officer fidgeting. A modern emergency vehicle runs one speaker off an amplifier carrying a handful of preset tones, and each tone exists for a different problem. Learn which is which and a passing siren turns into something you can read.

## The Four Sounds a Police Siren Makes

**Wail** is the slow one: a long sweep up and back down, a second or two per cycle. It carries furthest, which suits open road and distance, and it is the sound almost everyone pictures when they picture a siren.

**Yelp** is that same sweep run several times faster. Compressed like that it stops reading as a warning and starts reading as urgency, so it comes on close to traffic and at junctions.

**Hi-lo**, sometimes labelled phaser or piercer, alternates between two fixed notes rather than sweeping between them. Against a stationary queue of cars it cuts through better than a sweep does.

**Air horn** is the blunt instrument. It gets fired in short bursts over the top of whatever tone is already running, aimed at the one driver who still has not looked up.

## Why a Siren Is So Hard to Locate

Everybody has done the thing where you hear a siren, check three directions and find it in the fourth. That is not carelessness on your part. Traditional sirens work in a band of roughly 500 Hz to 1.8 kHz, and that band lands in an awkward gap for human hearing.

We locate low sounds mostly by the tiny difference in arrival time between our two ears, a cue that starts going ambiguous above about 1 kHz. Above that we lean on the difference in loudness between the ears instead, because your skull shadows the far one, but that cue is not pronounced enough to be much help below roughly 3 kHz. A siren tone sits at the bottom edge of the gap where neither mechanism is at its best. Then add a street of hard vertical surfaces throwing the sound around. Purpose-built directional sirens attack this by spreading energy across a much wider bandwidth, giving your ears more to work with.

## The Low Rumble You Feel Before You Hear

Sirens have a physics problem in cities. High frequencies bounce off glass and steel rather than passing through, so a driver with the windows up and music on may honestly not hear one coming. Federal Signal's answer, developed in 2007 and trialled by the NYPD that year, was the Rumbler. It listens to the main siren and re-emits it roughly two octaves down, in a band between about 182 and 400 Hz, through its own pair of speakers.

Low frequencies get through car doors and windscreens far more easily. They also shake things. Drivers describe feeling a Rumbler through the wheel and the seat before identifying it as a siren at all. It sounds for eight seconds and then shuts itself off, and it runs about ten decibels quieter than the siren it accompanies. Volume was never the point. Penetration was.

## Hi-Lo Means Something Different in California

The alternating two-note hi-lo is standard across much of Europe and has always read to American ears as foreign. California gave it a second job. After the 2017 Tubbs Fire exposed how badly first responders needed a way to tell an entire street to leave immediately, the state passed Senate Bill 909 in September 2020, authorising emergency vehicles to use a hi-lo tone for that one purpose and no other.

Counties from Humboldt to Ventura have fitted it since. If you live in one of them and you hear that particular alternating note, it is not a patrol car passing through. It means go.

## Why Older Sirens Sound Different

Before amplifiers and loudspeakers, a siren was a machine: a motor spinning a slotted rotor inside a slotted housing, chopping air into a tone. Federal Signal's Q-Siren, designed in 1951, had become the default on American fire apparatus by the 1970s and is still fitted today, because a mechanical siren has a thickness that a loudspeaker struggles to counterfeit. It is also punishingly loud, and noise complaints have pushed some departments to retire theirs.
